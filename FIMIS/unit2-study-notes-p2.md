## 1. Overview & Foundations of Financial Regulation (Slides 01–04)

### 1.1 Meaning & Scope of Financial Regulation
Financial regulation encompasses the body of laws, statutory rules, administrative guidelines, and prudential standards that govern the establishment, operational conduct, financial soundness, and supervision of financial institutions, markets, instruments, and intermediaries [497]. Its primary goal is to ensure that the financial system operates safely, transparently, fairly, and efficiently [497].

> 🧠 **Must Understand: Regulation is Continuous Oversight, Not a Static Rulebook**  
> Financial regulation is not a one-time checklist or a static policy document. Because financial entities handle public deposits and manage systemic risks, a failure in one institution can trigger a domino effect (contagion) across the entire economy [497, 498]. Regulation therefore manifests as **continuous oversight**—encompassing licensing, off-site monitoring, on-site inspection, capital adequacy verification, and enforcement actions whenever rules are breached [497].

---

### 1.2 The 6 Pillars of Financial Regulation (Need & Importance)
Every modern economy—including households, commercial enterprises, and governments—relies fundamentally on the financial system [498]. The necessity of financial regulation is grounded in **6 core pillars** [498, 499]:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        THE 6 PILLARS OF FINANCIAL REGULATION                      |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| 1. Protecting Consumers & Investors| Guards savers against fraud, mis-selling, &   |
|                                    | predatory practices [498, 499].               |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| 2. Ensuring Financial Stability    | Prevents localized defaults (e.g. bank runs) |
|                                    | from cascading into systemic crisis [499].   |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| 3. Maintaining Market Integrity    | Eradicates price manipulation, insider       |
|                                    | trading, & fraudulent practices [499].       |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| 4. Promoting Fair Competition      | Stops dominant entities from abusing market  |
|                                    | power & maintains an open playing field [499].|
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| 5. Channeling Savings Productively | Directs household savings toward high-return, |
|                                    | productive national investments [500].       |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
| 6. Building Public Confidence      | Sustains the bedrock of trust required for   |
|                                    | continuous saving, lending, & trading [500]. |
+------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
```

1. **Protecting Consumers & Investors**: Savers and retail investors possess asymmetrical information compared to sophisticated financial intermediaries. Regulation guards them against fraudulent schemes, mis-selling (e.g., unauthorized risk products), and unfair contract terms [498, 499].
2. **Ensuring Financial Stability**: Financial institutions are deeply interconnected through interbank markets and settlement systems. Regulation prevents localized insolvencies from triggering widespread contagion across the macroeconomic landscape [499].
3. **Maintaining Market Integrity**: Markets require transparent price discovery. Regulatory oversight prevents insider trading, market rigging, and price manipulation so that market prices accurately reflect genuine economic information [499].
4. **Promoting Fair Competition**: Regulation prevents monopolistic consolidation, ensuring that new, innovative, and smaller entrants can compete fairly without being squeezed out by dominant market players [499].
5. **Channeling Savings Productively**: By enforcing disclosures and prudential capital rules, regulation ensures that scarce national household savings flow into viable, growth-inducing capital investments rather than speculative bubbles [500].
6. **Building Public Confidence**: Money and credit operate entirely on trust. If public confidence collapses, depositors withdraw capital, liquidity freezes, and economic activity halts [500].

---

### 1.3 Statutory Regulators vs. Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs)
India employs a hybrid regulatory architecture consisting of **Statutory Authorities** armed with legislative powers and legal enforceability, complemented by **Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs)** and industry associations that drive self-discipline and operational standardization [500, 518, 519, 522].

| Dimension | Statutory Regulators (e.g., RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA) | Self-Regulatory Organizations / Industry Bodies (e.g., AMFI, AMBI/AIBI) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Basis** | Created by specific Acts of Parliament (statutory authority) [500, 519]. | Registered as non-profit societies or voluntary industry associations [153, 519, 522]. |
| **Primary Mandate** | Public interest, financial stability, investor protection, and market regulation [497, 519]. | Industry growth, standardizing best practices, codes of conduct, and member representation [518, 519, 522]. |
| **Enforcement Powers** | Can issue legally binding circulars, conduct searches/seizures, impose heavy monetary fines, suspend licenses, and initiate criminal prosecution [158, 176, 519]. | Lacks statutory penalty powers; enforces compliance through moral suasion, peer pressure, membership revocation, and code of ethics adherence [519, 522]. |
| **Relationship** | Accountable directly to the Parliament and the Ministry of Finance [159, 519]. | Operates under the supervisory oversight and guidance of statutory regulators [153, 518, 522]. |

## 2. Reserve Bank of India — RBI (Slides 05–11)

### 2.1 Origins, History & Statutory Evolution
The Reserve Bank of India stands as the central bank of the nation, the nerve center of the monetary system, and a principal member of international financial bodies like the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) [198, 222, 501].

#### Conceptual Roots & Commission Recommendations
- **Dr. B. R. Ambedkar's Conceptual Framework**: The structural concepts and operational principles of the RBI were derived from Dr. B. R. Ambedkar's seminal 1923 thesis book, *"The Problem of the Rupee — Its Origin and Its Solution"*, which laid out the mechanism for maintaining currency stability and central credit control [502].
- **Hilton Young Commission (1926)**: The Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance (headed by Edward Hilton Young) formally recommended in 1926 the creation of a central bank—to be called the Reserve Bank of India—to separate the control of currency and credit from the Government [502].

#### Timeline of Statutory Milestones
```
1934 ----------------> 1935 ----------------> 1937 ----------------> 1949
RBI Act Enacted        Commenced Operations   Permanently Shifted   Nationalized
(Legislative Framework) on April 1 (Calcutta) to Mumbai            (Fully GoI-owned)
```
1. **1934**: The British Colonial Administration enacted the **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** [502].
2. **April 1, 1935**: The RBI formally commenced operations as a privately-owned shareholders' bank with an initial paid-up capital of ₹5 crore, headquartered in **Calcutta** [502].
3. **1937**: The Central Office of the RBI was permanently relocated from Calcutta to **Mumbai** [502].
4. **January 1, 1949**: Following independence, the RBI was **nationalized** under the Reserve Bank (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act, 1948, transforming it into a 100% state-owned institution [198, 502].

### 2.3 Four Functional Roles of the RBI
The RBI executes four overarching functional mandates across the financial ecosystem [504-507]:

```
                                  +-----------------------+
                                  | FOUR FUNCTIONAL ROLES |
                                  +-----------+-----------+
                                              |
         +--------------------+---------------+---------------+--------------------+
         |                    |                               |                    |
+--------v-------+   +--------v-------+              +--------v-------+   +--------v-------+
| 1. Banking     |   | 2. NBFCs &     |              | 3. Monetary    |   | 4. Stability & |
| Regulation &   |   | Payment        |              | Policy & Forex |   | Development    |
| Supervision    |   | Systems        |              | Management     |   | Mandate        |
+----------------+   +----------------+              +----------------+   +----------------+
```

#### Role 1: Banking Regulation & Supervision
- **Licensing & Branch Expansion**: Issues operational licenses to Commercial Banks, RRBs, Small Finance Banks (SFBs), Payments Banks, and Urban Cooperative Banks under Section 22 of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 [53, 504].
- **Prudential Framework**: Enforces minimum Capital Adequacy Ratios (CRAR / Basel III), Risk-Weighted Asset (RWA) guidelines, exposure limits, and Income Recognition, Asset Classification, and Provisioning (IRACP) norms [63, 504].
- **On-Site & Off-Site Inspection**: Conducts annual financial inspections (CAMELS framework) and off-site surveillance to catch stress early [209, 504].

#### Role 2: Regulation of NBFCs & Payment Systems
- **NBFC Regulation**: Regulates Non-Banking Financial Companies under Chapter III-B of the RBI Act, 1934, setting Net Owned Fund (NOF) requirements, leverage caps, and asset-liability management (ALM) frameworks [135, 208, 505].
- **Payment & Settlement Systems**: Governs all retail and wholesale payment systems (UPI, NEFT, RTGS, Credit/Debit cards, PPIs) under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 (PSSA) [505].

#### Role 3: Monetary Policy & Forex Management
- **Monetary Policy Transmission**: Formulates monetary policy via the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) to maintain price stability while supporting economic growth [199, 506]. Utilizes direct tools (CRR, SLR) and indirect liquidity instruments (Repo rate, Reverse Repo, MSF, Open Market Operations) [20, 506].
- **Forex Reserves & Exchange Rate**: Manages India's foreign exchange reserves (Gold, SDRs, foreign currency assets) and ensures orderly forex market conduct under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA) [212, 506].

#### Role 4: Financial Stability & Development Mandate
- **Macro-Prudential Oversight**: Identifies systemic vulnerabilities across interconnected financial markets to prevent systemic contagion [19, 506].
- **Lender of Last Resort (LOLR)**: Extends emergency liquidity assistance to solvent banking institutions facing temporary liquidity crunches [426, 506].
- **Financial Inclusion & Innovation**: Drives developmental initiatives like Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY), priority sector lending (PSL) targets, and modern fintech/CBDC (Digital Rupee) pilots [2, 507].

### 2.4 Deep Case Studies: RBI Regulatory Interventions

#### 1. PMC Bank Fraud (2019)
- **Background**: Punjab and Maharashtra Cooperative (PMC) Bank was a multi-state Scheduled Urban Cooperative Bank with ₹11,617 crore in deposits and 137 branches [475, 476].
- **The Core Crisis**: The bank secretly lent over ₹6,226 crore—accounting for **73.5% of its total loan book**—to a single bankrupt real estate firm, HDIL (Housing Development and Infrastructure Ltd.) [481].
- **Deception Mechanism**: To hide this massive concentration risk from RBI inspectors, the bank's management created **21,049 dummy loan accounts** in its core banking system, masking HDIL's defaults and reporting an artificial NPA of just 2.19% (when actual NPAs exceeded 60-70%) [481, 483].
- **Exploitation of Dual Regulation**: PMC Bank took advantage of the regulatory loophole where administrative and board management was supervised by the Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS), while RBI only oversaw banking operations [483].
- **RBI Action**: On September 23, 2019, RBI invoked Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, superseded the board, appointed an administrator, and imposed strict deposit withdrawal caps (initially ₹1,000, later raised to ₹1,000,000) [478, 485]. PMC Bank was eventually amalgamated into **Unity Small Finance Bank** (jointly promoted by Centrum and BharatPe) [489].
- **Regulatory Reform**: Catalyzed the **Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020**, which brought all UCBs directly under RBI's supervision regarding management, board supersedure, audit, and capital raising [486-488]. DICGC deposit insurance was subsequently raised from ₹1 lakh to ₹5 lakh [485].

#### 2. Yes Bank Reconstruction Scheme (2020)
- **Background**: Yes Bank was the 4th largest private sector commercial bank in India.
- **The Core Crisis**: Severe governance failure, aggressive corporate lending to stressed groups (DHFL, IL&FS, Reliance ADAG), escalating Non-Performing Assets (NPAs), and continuous inability to raise fresh equity capital led to a massive run on deposits [508].
- **RBI Action**: In March 2020, to prevent systemic contagion, the RBI placed Yes Bank under a 30-day moratorium, superseded its board, appointed an administrator (ex-CFO of SBI, Prashant Kumar), and formulated the **Yes Bank Reconstruction Scheme, 2020** [508].
- **Resolution Strategy**: RBI orchestrated an unprecedented multi-institution bailout where a State Bank of India (SBI)-led consortium (including HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank) infused over ₹10,000 crore in fresh equity capital [508]. SBI acquired a 48% stake with a mandatory 3-year lock-in period for 75% of the shares, successfully protecting 100% of depositor funds without tapping taxpayer money [508].

#### 3. Dhanlaxmi Bank & Prompt Corrective Action (PCA)
- **Background**: Dhanlaxmi Bank, an old private sector bank based in Kerala, suffered from rapid unbridled expansion, sharp deterioration in asset quality, escalating cost-to-income ratios, and ongoing board-shareholder conflicts [508].
- **The Core Crisis**: Depleted Capital Adequacy Ratios (CRAR fell near statutory minimums) and elevated Net NPA ratios threatened bank solvency [508].
- **RBI Action**: The RBI placed Dhanlaxmi Bank under its **Prompt Corrective Action (PCA)** framework [508].
- **PCA Enforcement Mandatory Restrictions**:
  1. *Capital & Dividend*: Complete restriction on paying dividends or repatriating profits [508].
  2. *Branch Expansion*: Prohibition on opening new branches or expanding operational footprints [508].
  3. *Lending Restrictions*: Higher credit risk margins, restrictions on expanding exposure to unrated or high-risk corporate borrowers [508].
  4. *Management Compensation*: Caps on executive compensation and director fees [508].
- **Outcome**: The PCA framework forced Dhanlaxmi Bank to conserve capital, lower operating expenses, recover bad loans, and restore CRAR above threshold levels before restrictions were gradually eased [508].

## 3. Securities and Exchange Board of India — SEBI (Slides 12–17)

### 3.1 Genesis, Statutory Status & Core Mandate

#### Historical Evolution
During the 1980s, the Indian capital market experienced unprecedented growth in retail participation. However, this growth was accompanied by widespread market abuses—including rampant price rigging, unofficial kerb trading, unofficial badla deferrals, insider trading, and non-redressal of investor grievances [157, 509]. The existing regulatory architecture under the Controller of Capital Issues (CCI) was fragmented and administrative [158, 298].

- **Initial Establishment (1988)**: SEBI was initially constituted on **April 12, 1988**, as a non-statutory administrative body under the Ministry of Finance [25, 157, 509].
- **Statutory Conferment (1992)**: Following the outbreak of the multicrore Harshad Mehta securities scam in early 1992, the Government recognized that an administrative body lacked legal enforceability [157]. On **January 30, 1992**, SEBI was granted statutory powers via an Presidential Ordinance, which was formally enacted by Parliament as the **Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992** on **April 4, 1992** [25, 158, 159, 509].
- **Abolition of CCI**: Concurrent with statutory powers to SEBI, the Capital Issues (Control) Act, 1947 was repealed, abolishing the office of the Controller of Capital Issues (CCI) and ushering in an era of free market pricing for capital issues [158, 298].
- **Current Leadership**: **Shri Tuhin Kanta Pandey** (assumed charge as SEBI Chairperson on March 1, 2025) [510].

---

### 3.2 The 4 Core Objectives of SEBI
Section 11(1) of the SEBI Act mandates a four-fold core objective for the regulator [156, 158, 509, 510]:

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                            THE 4 CORE OBJECTIVES OF SEBI                        |
+------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
| 1. Investor Protection             | Safeguarding the rights & interests of     |
|                                    | retail & institutional investors [509].    |
+------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
| 2. Preventing Malpractices         | Eliminating insider trading, price rigging,|
|                                    | & fraudulent trade practices [510].        |
+------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
| 3. Code of Conduct for Intermediaries| Regulating brokers, underwriters, merchant |
|                                    | bankers, & portfolio managers [510].       |
+------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
| 4. Balancing Regulation & SROs     | Combining statutory oversight with industry|
|                                    | self-regulatory mechanisms [510].          |
+------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
```

1. **Investor Protection**: Protecting the rights and investments of investors by ensuring full, fair, and timely disclosures, transparent allotment mechanisms, and effective grievance redressal [158, 509].
2. **Preventing Malpractices**: Eradicating unfair, fraudulent, and manipulative trade practices, including price manipulation, insider trading, and unauthorized market cornering [158, 510].
3. **Code of Conduct for Intermediaries**: Professionalizing capital market intermediaries (brokers, merchant bankers, underwriters, debenture trustees, registrars) by enforcing strict registration criteria, net worth requirements, and operational codes of conduct [158, 510].
4. **Balancing Regulation & Self-Regulation**: Achieving an optimum balance between overarching statutory regulation by SEBI and operational self-regulation by SROs (such as recognized stock exchanges, AMFI, and AMBI) [158, 510].

### 3.3 The 4 Governing Acts of the Indian Capital Market
The legal architecture governing the securities market in India rests upon **4 primary Acts of Parliament** [156, 512]:

```
                     +--------------------------------------------+
                     | 4 GOVERNING ACTS OF THE CAPITAL MARKET     |
                     +---------------------+----------------------+
                                           |
         +--------------------+------------+------------+--------------------+
         |                    |                         |                    |
+--------v-------+   +--------v-------+        +--------v-------+   +--------v-------+
| 1. SEBI Act,   |   | 2. Companies   |        | 3. SCRA, 1956  |   | 4. Depositories|
| 1992           |   | Act, 1956/2013 |        |                |   | Act, 1996      |
+----------------+   +----------------+        +----------------+   +----------------+
```

1. **SEBI Act, 1992**: Establishes SEBI with statutory powers to protect investors, develop the securities market, regulate market intermediaries, issue cease-and-desist directions (Section 11B), and impose monetary penalties (up to ₹25 crore or 3x ill-gotten profits) [156, 176, 512].
2. **Companies Act, 1956 / 2013**: Governs the issue, allotment, and transfer of securities, prospectus disclosure standards, corporate governance requirements, mandatory auditor rotation, rights/bonus issues, and dividend declarations [156, 512].
3. **Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA)**: Regulates trading in securities, grants recognition to stock exchanges and clearing corporations, governs listing agreements, and prevents undesirable speculative transactions [156, 512].
4. **Depositories Act, 1996**: Provides the legal framework for establishing electronic depositories (NSDL, CDSL), dematerializing physical share certificates, and enabling seamless, electronic, paperless transfer of beneficial ownership [156, 512].

### 3.5 Core Regulatory Functions of SEBI
Under Section 11 of the SEBI Act, SEBI carries out three broad categories of functions: **Protective, Regulatory, and Developmental** [161-163, 515, 516]:

#### 1. Protective Functions
- **Prohibiting Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices (FUTP)**: Enforces SEBI (PFUTP) Regulations, 2003 to penalize wash sales, circular trading, and artificial price rigging [166, 515].
- **Prohibiting Insider Trading**: Enforces SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015, mandating strict disclosures and trading windows for connected persons possessing Unpublished Price Sensitive Information (UPSI) [163, 515].
- **Investor Education**: Educates retail investors through nationwide financial literacy drives and manages the SCORES (SEBI Complaints Redress System) online grievance portal [182, 183, 515].

#### 2. Regulatory Functions
- **Registration & Regulation of Intermediaries**: Registers stockbrokers, sub-brokers, merchant bankers, portfolio managers, underwriters, credit rating agencies, and DPs [161, 170, 515].
- **Regulation of Collective Investment Schemes & Mutual Funds**: Enforces SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, laying down investment limits, valuation norms, and AMC governance [147, 163, 516].
- **Corporate Governance & Listing**: Enforces SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 (LODR), mandating quarterly disclosures, independent director ratios, audit committees, and related-party transaction approvals [166, 515].

#### 3. Developmental Functions
- **Market Infrastructure Modernization**: Introduced electronic screen-based trading (BOLT/NEAT), T+1 settlement cycles, dematerialization, and ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) for IPOs [186, 193, 516].
- **Promoting SROs & Innovation**: Frameworks for SME Exchanges, REITs, InvITs, Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), and sandbox environments for fintech testing [34, 166, 516].

### 3.6 Evolution & Landmark Case Studies: Regulatory Shift Timeline

#### 1. Harshad Mehta Securities Scam (1992)
- **The Scam**: Exploited structural loopholes in the banking system using fake **Bank Receipts (BRs)** and ready-forward (repo) deals to siphon over ₹4,000–₹5,000 crore from commercial banks into the stock market, artificially pumping targeted scrips (e.g., ACC) [23, 510, 511].
- **SEBI/Regulatory Shift**: Prompted the immediate enactment of the **SEBI Act, 1992** (conferring statutory powers), repeal of the Capital Issues (Control) Act, 1947, abolition of the Controller of Capital Issues (CCI), and establishment of the National Stock Exchange (NSE) as a screen-based demutualized exchange [23, 158, 511].

#### 2. Ketan Parekh Scam (2001)
- **The Scam**: Ketan Parekh formed a cartel to execute circular trading, pump-and-dump schemes, and artificial price rigging in 10 tech/telecom stocks (the "K-10" scrips) using massive funds siphoned from Madhavpura Mercantile Cooperative Bank and Pay-order borrowings [24, 511].
- **SEBI/Regulatory Shift**: Banned the centuries-old **Badla (carry-forward) trading system** permanently in July 2001 [24, 188, 511]. Introduced **T+2 rolling settlements**, VaR (Value at Risk) risk-based margining, intraday price bands/circuit filters, and mandatory client codes [26, 186, 511].

#### 3. Satyam Accounting Fraud (2009) & Sahara OFCD Case (2012)
- **The Scam**: Satyam Computers' Chairman B. Ramalinga Raju confessed to a ₹7,800 crore accounting fraud involving falsified bank deposits, phantom interest income, and fake employees [511]. In Sahara, two unlisted Sahara group companies raised over ₹24,000 crore from 3 crore retail investors via Optionally Fully Convertible Debentures (OFCDs) without complying with public issue listing norms [511].
- **SEBI/Regulatory Shift**: SEBI issued comprehensive **LODR Regulations, 2015**, mandatory rotation of statutory auditors, strict independent director norms under Companies Act 2013, mandatory whistleblower mechanisms, and obtained statutory powers to order **disgorgement of ill-gotten profits** and attach bank accounts [166, 176, 511].

#### 4. Digital-Era Enforcement (2024–2026)
- **The Scam**: Emergence of unregulated "finfluencers" executing pump-and-dump schemes via social media (Telegram/YouTube), insider trading in Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) scrips, and high-frequency algorithmic trading manipulative strategies (e.g., ₹4,843 crore regulatory enforcement action against Jane Street) [512].
- **SEBI/Regulatory Shift**: SEBI barred regulated entities from associating with unregistered finfluencers, introduced strict HFT algorithmic trading audits, enhanced digital surveillance using AI/data analytics, and enforced stringent cross-border regulatory actions [512].

### 4.3 Section 14: Duties, Powers & Functions
Section 14 of the IRDA Act, 1999 lays down the statutory powers and operational responsibilities of the authority [147, 569]:

1. **Policyholder Protection**: Framing guidelines on policy assignments, nomination rights, insurable interest, claim settlement procedures, surrender values, and preventing unfair policy terms [147, 569].
2. **Registration & Licensing**: Sole authority to issue, renew, modify, suspend, or cancel registration certificates for life, general, and health insurers and reinsurers [147, 148, 473].
3. **Codes of Conduct & Intermediary Supervision**: Specifying qualifications, code of conduct, and training requirements for insurance agents, corporate agents, brokers, surveyors, loss assessors, and Third-Party Administrators (TPAs) [147, 503, 569].
4. **Prudential & Solvency Norms**: Mandating control of solvency margins (minimum 150% Solvency Ratio), capital adequacy, and accounting disclosures [146, 490].
5. **Investment Regulations (Section 27A/27D)**: Dictating pattern of policyholder fund deployment—requiring minimum 50% in Government & Approved Securities and minimum 15% in Infrastructure & Social sectors, while prohibiting investment outside India [153, 467, 477, 491, 494].
6. **Reinsurance Oversight**: Regulating cessions, retention limits, and mandatory cessions to national reinsurers (e.g., GIC Re) [482, 484, 569].
7. **Rural & Social Sector Obligations**: Enforcing statutory targets for insurers to write a minimum percentage of business in rural areas and unorganized social sectors [148, 155, 479, 569].

### 5.2 PFRDA: Genesis, Mandate & NPS vs. APY Comparison

#### Genesis & Mandate
Established in **2003** by an executive resolution and granted full statutory status under the **PFRDA Act, 2013** (enacted 2014), the **Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA)** is headquartered in **New Delhi** [574].
- **Current Leadership**: Shri Siva Subramanian Ramann (Chairperson) [575].
- **Primary Objective**: To promote, regulate, and supervise pension funds in India, ensuring financial security post-retirement [3, 4, 574].

#### Key Regulated Schemes: NPS vs. APY

   - Launched in **2004** for government employees; opened to **all citizens** (18–70 years) voluntarily in **2009** [121, 575].

### 5.3 NHB & Housing Shift: Pre-2019 Conflict, 2019 DHFL Crisis & RBI Takeover

#### Genesis & Core Mandate of NHB
Set up under the **National Housing Bank Act, 1987** (began operations July 1988), the **National Housing Bank (NHB)** was established as the apex institution for housing finance [192, 576]. Its functions covered:
1. **Promotional & Developmental**: Fostering Housing Finance Companies (HFCs) and low-income housing [194, 577].
2. **Refinance**: Extending liquidity support to HFCs, banks, and RRBs against housing loans [196, 577].
3. **Prudential Regulation**: Regulating deposit-taking and capital adequacy of HFCs [195, 577].
4. **Secondary Mortgage Market**: Launching Residential Mortgage-Backed Securities (RMBS) and NHB RESIDEX [206, 213, 577].

#### The Pre-2019 Conflict & DHFL Crisis Trigger
- **Pre-2019 Structural Flaw**: NHB functioned simultaneously as **lender/refinancer** to HFCs and as their **primary regulator** [578]. A lender policing its own borrowers created an inherent **conflict of interest**, resulting in "light-touch" regulatory supervision [578].
- **2019 DHFL Crisis**: Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Ltd (DHFL) defaulted on debt obligations in 2019 due to extreme Asset-Liability Mismatch (funding 20-year home loans using 90-day Commercial Papers) [189, 578]. The crisis exposed the regulatory vacuum in HFC supervision [578].

### 6.2 Common Exam Confusions & Trap Alerts

> ✍️ **Exam Focus: Avoid These 5 Traps**  
> 1. **Trap 1: SRO vs. Statutory Regulator**: AMFI and AMBI/AIBI are **Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs)** with **no statutory penalty powers** [572, 580]. Penalties, suspensions, and regulatory enforcement for mutual funds or merchant bankers are executed exclusively by **SEBI** [572, 580].  
> 2. **Trap 2: HFC Regulation Pre- vs. Post-2019**: Prior to August 2019, NHB was both regulator and refinancer of HFCs [578]. Following the **DHFL default**, the **Finance Act, 2019** transferred direct regulatory authority over HFCs to the **RBI**, leaving NHB focused on refinancing, development, and inspection [579].  
> 3. **Trap 3: PFRDA Statutory Status Year**: PFRDA was established by executive resolution in **2003**, but received statutory status under the **PFRDA Act, 2013** (enacted 2014) [574].  
> 4. **Trap 4: SEBI Statutory Status Year**: SEBI was set up as an administrative body in **1988**, but became an autonomous statutory regulator under the **SEBI Act, 1992** (following the Harshad Mehta scam) [158, 508].  
> 5. **Trap 5: NPS vs. APY Benefits**: NPS returns are **market-linked** (variable based on equity/bond performance) [575], whereas APY provides a **guaranteed fixed minimum pension** (₹1,000–₹5,000/month) [576].
