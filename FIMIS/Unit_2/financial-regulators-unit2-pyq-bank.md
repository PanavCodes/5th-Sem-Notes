##### 1. Definition & Rationale of Financial Regulation
Financial regulation encompasses the laws, rules, administrative guidelines, and prudential standards that govern the establishment, conduct, and supervision of financial institutions, markets, instruments, and intermediaries [404]. Its primary goal is to ensure that the financial system functions safely, fairly, and efficiently [404]. Regulation is an **ongoing system of continuous oversight** (licensing, inspection, off-site surveillance) rather than a static rulebook [404].

##### 2. The 6 Pillars of Financial Regulation (Need & Importance)
The necessity of financial regulation rests on **6 core pillars**:
1. **Protecting Consumers & Investors**: Guards retail savers against asymmetric information, fraud, predatory lending, and mis-selling of financial products [404].
2. **Ensuring Financial Stability**: Prevents localized insolvencies (e.g., bank runs) from cascading into systemic panic and contagion across the economy [404].
3. **Maintaining Market Integrity**: Eradicates price manipulation, insider trading, and fraudulent market practices to ensure transparent price discovery [404].
4. **Promoting Fair Competition**: Prevents dominant financial entities from abusing market power and ensures an open, competitive playing field [404].
5. **Channeling Savings Productively**: Directs household savings into productive, growth-inducing capital investments rather than speculative asset bubbles [404].
6. **Building Public Confidence**: Sustains public trust, which is the indispensable bedrock of modern fractional-reserve banking and capital markets [404].

##### 3. Statutory Regulators vs. Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs)

| Dimension | Statutory Regulators (e.g., RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA) | Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs) / Industry Bodies (e.g., AMFI, AMBI) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Status** | Created by specific Acts of Parliament (Statutory Bodies) [404]. | Registered as non-profit societies or voluntary industry associations [404, 414, 417]. |
| **Statutory Powers** | Possess full legal powers to frame binding rules, conduct searches/seizures, levy fines, and cancel licenses [404, 415]. | Lack statutory penalty powers; enforce compliance through moral suasion, codes of conduct, and peer discipline [404, 415]. |
| **Primary Focus** | Public interest, systemic stability, investor protection, and market regulation [404]. | Industry development, standardizing best practices, training, and member representation [404, 414, 417]. |
| **Accountability** | Accountable directly to Parliament and the Ministry of Finance [404]. | Operate under the supervisory guidance and oversight of statutory regulators [404, 414, 417]. |

##### 1. Genesis & Legal Authority of RBI
- **Origins**: The RBI's conceptual foundation was shaped by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar’s 1923 book *"The Problem of the Rupee — Its Origin and Its Solution"* and formally established based on the recommendations of the **Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance (Hilton Young Commission, 1926)** [405].
- **Key Dates**:
  - Enactment of the **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** [405].
  - Commenced operations on **1st April 1935** in Calcutta [405].
  - Permanently relocated to **Mumbai in 1937** [405].
  - **Nationalized on 1st January 1949** (originally a private shareholders' bank) [405].
- **Central Board Composition**: Governed by a Central Board of Directors (4-year term) comprising the Governor (executive head), up to 4 Deputy Governors, 2 Finance Ministry representatives, and 4 directors from local boards (Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, New Delhi) [406].
- **Historical Leadership Trivia**:
  - First Governor: **Sir Osborne Smith** [406].
  - First Indian Governor: **C. D. Deshmukh** [406].
  - First Woman Deputy Governor: **K. J. Udeshi** [406].
  - Only Prime Minister who served as RBI Governor: **Dr. Manmohan Singh** [406].
  - Current Governor: **Shri Sanjay Malhotra** (appointed late 2024/2025) [406].

##### 2. The 4 Core Functional Roles of the RBI
1. **Banking Regulation & Supervision**: Under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, RBI licenses commercial, cooperative, and regional rural banks; enforces prudential capital adequacy norms (CAR/CRAR); sets exposure limits; conducts CAMELS inspections; and protects depositors through PCA (Prompt Corrective Action) [405, 406].
2. **NBFC Oversight & Payment Systems Supervision**:
   - Regulates Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) under Chapter III-B of the RBI Act, 1934 [406].
   - Oversees National Payment Systems (NEFT, RTGS, UPI) under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, promoting digital transactions while ensuring cyber-security [406, 407].
3. **Forex Management & Monetary Policy Transmission**:
   - Administers the **Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999**, managing external trade, capital flows, and foreign exchange reserves [407].
   - Sets benchmark policy rates (Repo Rate, Reverse Repo Rate) via the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) to maintain price stability while supporting economic growth [407, 408].
4. **Financial Stability & Macro-Prudential Oversight**: Acts as the Lender of Last Resort to banks, addresses systemic liquidity shocks, and conducts stress-testing across financial institutions [408].

##### 3. Monetary Policy & Liquidity Management Instruments (Tackling Inflation)
The RBI utilizes both **Direct (Quantitative)** and **Indirect (Market-Based)** instruments to control money supply and tackle inflation [407, 408]:

| Instrument | Mechanism & Operating Function | Impact on Inflation & Liquidity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Repo Rate** (LAF) | Rate at which RBI lends short-term money to commercial banks against government securities [407, 408]. | **Raising Repo Rate** increases borrowing costs for banks, tightening credit supply and cooling inflation [407, 408]. |
| **Reverse Repo Rate** | Rate at which RBI absorbs excess overnight liquidity from commercial banks [407, 408]. | **Raising Reverse Repo** encourages banks to park idle surplus with RBI rather than extending risky credit [407, 408]. |
| **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)** | Mandatory percentage of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) banks must hold as cash reserves with RBI (Sec 42(1), RBI Act) [408]. | **Raising CRR** directly locks away bank funds, reducing the credit creation capacity of the banking system [408]. |
| **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** | Mandatory percentage of NDTL banks must invest in unencumbered government approved securities, gold, or cash (Sec 24, BR Act) [408]. | **Raising SLR** redirects bank capital toward government bonds, restricting private sector credit expansion [408]. |
| **Open Market Operations (OMOs)** | Outright purchase or sale of Central/State Government securities in the open market by RBI [408]. | **Selling G-Secs** siphons off excess liquidity from the market; **Buying G-Secs** injects fresh cash [408]. |

##### 1. Conceptual Meaning
- **Commercial Banks**: Financial intermediaries licensed to accept public deposits (savings, current, term) and grant short- to medium-term loans to individuals and businesses [397, 428].
- **Investment Banks**: Specialized non-bank financial institutions that deal in capital market operations, assisting corporations and governments in raising long-term capital through underwriting, equity/debt issue management, and M&A advisory [397, 428, 429].
- **Central Bank (RBI)**: The apex monetary and regulatory authority of the country, responsible for regulating currency, supervising the banking system, and maintaining monetary stability [397, 428, 429].

##### ✍️ Unified Model Answer (10 Marks)

##### 1. Case Study 1: PMC Bank Debacle (2019) — Governance Failure & Regulatory Response
- **Background**: Punjab and Maharashtra Cooperative (PMC) Bank was a top-10 Scheduled Urban Cooperative Bank (UCB) operating across 7 states [376, 377].
- **Core Scam**: PMC Bank's management colluded with the promoters of **Housing Development and Infrastructure Limited (HDIL)** [374, 381]. The bank extended ₹6,226 Crore (73% of its total loan book) to HDIL, far exceeding the statutory single-borrower exposure limit [382]. To hide this from RBI inspectors, the bank created over **21,000 dummy accounts** and under-reported NPAs (showing 2.19% instead of the actual 60–70%) [382].
- **RBI Action**: On 23rd September 2019, RBI invoked **Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949**, superseding the board, imposing a 6-month moratorium, and capping deposit withdrawals initially at ₹1,000 (later raised to ₹1 Lakh) [378, 385].
- **Regulatory Impact & Reforms**: Led directly to the **Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020**, bringing UCBs under direct RBI prudential supervision (board appointment, auditing, CRAR). PMC Bank was eventually resolved by merging it into **Unity Small Finance Bank** (promoted by Centrum & BharatPe) [374, 386].

##### 2. Case Study 2: Yes Bank Reconstruction Scheme (2020) — Systemic Rescue
- **Background**: Yes Bank experienced severe governance lapses, aggressive unhedged lending to stressed corporate groups, and spiraling NPAs, leading to an acute liquidity freeze and deposit run [408].
- **RBI Action**: On 5th March 2020, RBI imposed a moratorium, suspended the board, and drafted the **Yes Bank Reconstruction Scheme, 2020** [408].
- **Resolution Mechanism**: RBI orchestrated an institutional bailout led by **State Bank of India (SBI)** (which injected ₹7,250 Crore for a 49% stake) alongside a consortium of private banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, Kotak) [408]. AT-1 bonds worth ₹8,415 Crore were written down per Basel III norms [408].
- **Key Takeaway**: Demonstrated RBI's capacity as Lender of Last Resort to resolve a systemically important private bank without caller loss or depositor bail-in [408].

##### 3. Case Study 3: Dhanlaxmi Bank & Prompt Corrective Action (PCA)
- **Background**: Dhanlaxmi Bank (an old private sector bank) suffered persistent financial distress—rising NPAs, falling Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR), negative Return on Assets (ROA), and internal board tussles [408].
- **RBI Action**: RBI placed Dhanlaxmi Bank under the **Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework** [408].
- **Mechanisms**: Imposed strict restrictions on branch expansion, staff recruitment, high-risk commercial lending, and dividend distribution [408].
- **Key Takeaway**: PCA serves as an early-intervention structured governance framework to nurse weak banks back to health before complete capital insolvency [408].

##### 1. Genesis & Statutory Status of SEBI
- **Establishment**: SEBI was initially set up in **April 1988** as an administrative body under the Ministry of Finance [408].
- **Statutory Powers**: Following the 1992 Harshad Mehta securities scam, SEBI was granted statutory powers on **30th January 1992** through an Ordinance, followed by the enactment of the **Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992** (repealing the Capital Issues (Control) Act, 1947) [408, 410].

##### 2. The 4 Core Objectives of SEBI
1. **Investor Protection**: Protecting the rights and interests of investors in securities through education, disclosure mandates, and grievance redressal [408, 409].
2. **Preventing Malpractices**: Eradicating fraudulent trade practices, price rigging, insider trading, and cartelization [409].
3. **Regulating Market Intermediaries**: Establishing a binding code of conduct for brokers, underwriters, merchant bankers, portfolio managers, and registrars [409, 412].
4. **Promoting Market Development**: Balancing statutory regulation with self-regulation to foster an efficient, modern capital market infrastructure [409, 413].

##### 3. The 4 Governing Capital Market Acts
SEBI derives its regulatory authority from **4 core statutory Acts**:
1. **SEBI Act, 1992**: Grants statutory powers of registration, investigation, inspection, search/seizure, and penalty imposition [408, 410].
2. **Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act (SCRA), 1956**: Governs the recognition, demutualization, and operations of stock exchanges [410].
3. **Depositories Act, 1996**: Provides the legal framework for dematerialization of securities, scriptless trading, and depositories (NSDL/CDSL) [410].
4. **Companies Act, 2013 (Relevant Provisions)**: Empowers SEBI to regulate issue, allotment, and transfer of securities by listed/proposed-to-be-listed companies [410].

##### 4. Triple Functional Roles of SEBI
- **Protective Functions**: Prohibiting insider trading under SEBI (PIT) Regulations, banning Unfair Trade Practices (FUTP), operating the **SCORES 2.0** online grievance portal, and managing the **Investor Protection and Education Fund (IPEF)** [409, 411, 412].
- **Regulatory Functions**: Registering and supervising brokers, depositories, mutual funds, merchant bankers; enforcing **SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015** for corporate governance; and setting margin requirements [411, 412].
- **Developmental Functions**: Introducing innovative issue mechanisms like **ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount)**, promoting **UPI in IPOs**, enabling T+1 and T+0 settlement cycles, and training market intermediaries [410, 413].

##### 5. Two Specific Regulatory Tools Used by SEBI (10 Marks Requirement)
1. **Application Supported by Blocked Amount (ASBA)**: A mechanism whereby application money for public issues (IPOs) remains blocked in the investor's bank account until allotment, eliminating refund delays, fraud, and interest loss for retail investors [410].
2. **Index-Based Market-Wide Circuit Breakers**: Automatic trading halts triggered at 10%, 15%, and 20% movements in BSE Sensex or NSE Nifty to curb extreme market volatility, panic selling, and manipulative bear hammering [410, 412].

#### 📌 Question Group 3.2: SEBI Evolution & Landmark Case Studies
**Priority Tag**: 🔥 **Very Important** (High-Probable Application Case Studies)  
**Bloom's Level**: BL-4 (Analyze) / BL-5 (Evaluate)  

**Exam Questions Included in this Group**:
1. *Expected / Predicted Syllabus Question*: "Examine the evolution of SEBI through landmark financial scams and digital-era enforcement cases: (a) Harshad Mehta Scam (1992), (b) Ketan Parekh Scam (2001), (c) Satyam & Sahara Cases, and (d) Digital Era Enforcement (Jane Street ₹4,843 Cr, IEX Price Discovery, Finfluencer Crackdown)." `[Marks: 10 | Syllabus Scope: Unit 2.pptx, Slides 13–14 & 17]` [409, 410, 413]

---

##### ✍️ Unified Model Answer (10 Marks)

##### 1. Harshad Mehta Scam (1992) — Trigger for Statutory Status
- **Mechanism**: Diverted public sector bank funds into the stock market using fake Bank Receipts (BRs) and Ready Forward (Repo) deals, driving the BSE Sensex from 1,168 to 4,285 points in a single year [409, 410].
- **Regulatory Reform**: Exposed the complete absence of capital market regulation. Led directly to the enactment of the **SEBI Act, 1992**, abolishing the Controller of Capital Issues (CCI) and giving statutory powers to SEBI [410].

##### 2. Ketan Parekh Scam (2001) — Reform of Trading Mechanics
- **Mechanism**: Manipulated "K-10" technology/telecom stocks using circular trading, badla funding, and fraudulent pay orders from Madhavpura Mercantile Cooperative Bank [410].
- **Regulatory Reform**: SEBI completely **banned the Badla deferred settlement system**, introduced **compulsory dematerialization**, implemented **T+2 rolling settlement**, and mandated **index-based circuit filters** [410].

##### 3. Satyam (2009) & Sahara (2012) Cases — Corporate Governance overhaul
- **Satyam Case (2009)**: ₹7,800 Crore accounting fraud (fabricated bank balances and interest) [410]. *Reform*: SEBI mandated compulsory auditor rotation, independent director norms, and introduced **SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015** [410, 411].
- **Sahara Case (2012)**: Raised over ₹24,000 Crore from 3 Crore retail investors through Optionally Fully Convertible Debentures (OFCDs) without complying with public issue norms [410]. *Reform*: SEBI enforced strict public issue threshold rules (any offer to 50+ persons is deemed a public issue) and recovered investor capital through Supreme Court-mandated disgorgement [410].

##### 4. Digital Era Enforcement Cases (2024–2025)
- **Jane Street Algo Trading Case**: SEBI impounded **₹4,843 Crore** in illegal gains from offshore high-frequency algorithmic trading strategies manipulating Indian equity derivatives [413].
- **Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) Intervention**: SEBI intervened in energy derivative price discovery to prevent artificial volume rigging [413].
- **Finfluencer & Unregistered Advisor Crackdown**: Banned unregistered financial influencers from sharing revenue with SEBI-regulated brokers/AMCs and levied heavy disgorgement orders on deceptive social media "stock gurus" [413].

---

#### 📌 Question Group 4.1: IRDAI Composition, Powers (Sec 14) & 2024–2025 Reforms
**Priority Tag**: ⭐ **Important** (Core Statutory Regulator Coverage)  
**Bloom's Level**: BL-2 (Understand) / BL-3 (Apply)  

**Exam Questions Included in this Group**:
1. *Expected / Predicted Syllabus Question*: "Discuss the statutory role, board composition under Section 4, duties and powers under Section 14 of the IRDAI Act, 1999. Highlight the key regulatory reforms introduced in 2024–2025." `[Marks: 10 | Syllabus Scope: Unit 2.pptx, Slides 18–20]` [413, 414]

---

##### ✍️ Unified Model Answer (10 Marks / 5 Marks)

##### 1. Genesis & Legal Status of IRDAI
- **Enactment**: Formed based on the **Malhotra Committee (1994)** recommendations; constituted as an autonomous statutory body under the **IRDAI Act, 1999** [413].
- **Headquarters Relocation**: Established in New Delhi; **permanently relocated to Hyderabad in 2001** to foster regional financial decentralization [413].
- **Core Mission**: Protect policyholder interests, ensure fair treatment, and foster orderly growth of the insurance and reinsurance industry in India [413, 414].

##### 2. Section 4: Composition of the Ten-Member Authority
Under Section 4 of the IRDAI Act, 1999, the Authority consists of a **10-member board** appointed by the Government of India [413]:
- **1 Chairman** (Executive Head) [413].
- **5 Full-Time Members** (handling Life, Non-Life, Actuarial, Finance/Investment, and Law) [413].
- **4 Part-Time Members** (representing government and industry expertise) [413].

##### 3. Section 14: Duties, Powers & Functions of IRDAI
Under Section 14 of the Act, IRDAI is empowered to [413, 414]:
1. **Licensing & Registration**: Issue, renew, modify, suspend, or cancel registration certificates for life, general, and health insurance companies and reinsurers [413, 414].
2. **Policyholder Protection**: Mandate terms, claim settlement timelines, and grievance redressal mechanisms for policyholders [413, 414].
3. **Intermediary Regulation**: Regulate qualifications, code of conduct, and commission structures for insurance agents, brokers, and surveyors [413, 414].
4. **Solvency Margin Oversight**: Prescribe and enforce minimum solvency margins to ensure insurers remain capital-solvent to pay future claims [414].
5. **Investment Pattern Control**: Mandate investment allocations of insurance funds into government securities, social sectors, and infrastructure [414].

##### 4. Key Landmark Reforms (2024–2025)
- **"Insurance for All by 2047" Vision**: Comprehensive master plan to achieve universal insurance coverage across India by the centenary of independence [414].
- **74% Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: Fully operationalized 74% FDI limit under the automatic route, encouraging global capital and technical underwriting expertise [414].
- **Regulatory Sandbox 2.0**: Enabled tech-driven insurtech startups to pilot innovative products (usage-based auto insurance, OPD health covers) in a live environment [414].
- **DPDP Act 2023 Compliance**: Mandated strict data privacy standards for customer medical and personal data [414].

#### 📌 Question Group 5.3: National Housing Bank (NHB) & Pre- vs. Post-2019 Regulatory Shift
**Priority Tag**: 🔥 **Very Important** (Major Structural Regulatory Shift)  
**Bloom's Level**: BL-2 (Understand) / BL-4 (Analyze)  

**Exam Questions Included in this Group**:
1. "National Housing Board (NHB) supports RBI. How does the effective functioning of NHB help RBI in effective functioning of the economy?" `[Marks: 5]`  
   — *Paper*: `Financial_Institutions__Markets__Instruments_and_Service__Sem-V__Year_2022-23__Special_Re_Exam_w8Kyx62H1c.pdf` (Date: 23 June 2023), **Q1a** [258]
2. *Expected / Predicted Syllabus Question*: "Critically examine the pre-2019 conflict of interest in NHB's mandate, the 2019 DHFL crisis trigger, and the transfer of Housing Finance Company (HFC) regulatory powers to the RBI under the Finance Act, 2019." `[Marks: 10 | Syllabus Scope: Unit 2.pptx, Slides 25–26]` [416, 417]

---

##### ✍️ Unified Model Answer (10 Marks / 5 Marks)

##### 1. Role & Genesis of NHB
- **Establishment**: Set up under the **National Housing Bank Act, 1987** as the apex institution for housing finance [416]. Operations commenced in July 1988 [416]. Headquartered in New Delhi [416].
- **Core Functions**: Refinances Housing Finance Companies (HFCs) and banks, promotes affordable housing schemes, publishes **NHB RESIDEX** (residential property price index), and develops mortgage securitization [416].

##### 2. Pre-2019 Conflict & The 2019 DHFL Trigger
- **Pre-2019 Dual Role Conflict**: Prior to 2019, NHB functioned simultaneously as the **Regulator of HFCs** and the **Primary Refinancer/Lender to HFCs** [417]. A lender policing its own borrowers created an inherent conflict of interest and led to light-touch regulation [417].
- **The Trigger (DHFL Crisis, 2019)**: Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited (DHFL) defaulted on short-term commercial paper and debt obligations due to severe asset-liability mismatches (funding 20-year housing loans with 90-day CPs) [417]. This exposed systemic vulnerabilities in HFC oversight [417].

##### 3. The 2019 Fix: RBI Regulatory Takeover
- **Legislative Amendment**: Through the **Finance Act, 2019** (effective 9th August 2019), Parliament amended the NHB Act, 1987, transferring all regulatory powers over HFCs from NHB to the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** [417].
- **Post-2019 Division of Labor**:
  - **RBI**: Functions as the sole regulator—setting prudential norms, LTV ratios, liquidity coverage, capital adequacy, and governance standards for HFCs (treating them as a specialized category of NBFCs) [417].
  - **NHB**: Retains its core developmental, refinancing, and on-site inspection/audit roles (similar to NABARD's relationship with Regional Rural Banks) [417].
