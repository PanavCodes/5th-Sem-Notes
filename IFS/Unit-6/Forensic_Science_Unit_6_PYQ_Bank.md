* **Original PYQs:**

  3. *"Compare Cybercrime and Conventional crime."* (SVKMs NMIMS MPSTME, Sem-VI Final Exam 2023–24, Date: 02 May 2024, Q2-c, **Marks: 6**, CO-3; SO-6; BL-2) [`Introduction_to_Forensic_Science__Sem_VI__Final_Exam_2023-24_cXHk2b4kTT.pdf`, Slide 4–5]
* **Priority Level:** 🔥 **Very Important** (Tested in 3 separate university exam sessions)

##### Model Answer (2 to 6 Marks):

**1. Cyber-Enabled vs. Cyber-Dependent Crimes (2 Marks Definition):**
* **Cyber-Enabled Crimes:** Traditional physical crimes (e.g., financial fraud, extortion, harassment, identity theft) that are scaled up, accelerated, or facilitated through the use of computers or the Internet [Slide 3; Saferstein, Ch. 19].
* **Cyber-Dependent Crimes:** Offenses that exist exclusively in the digital environment and can only be committed using ICT systems (e.g., hacking, developing and distributing malware, ransomware attacks, and Distributed Denial of Service [DDoS] attacks) [Slide 3; Saferstein, Ch. 19].

**2. Detailed Comparison: Conventional Crime vs. Computer Crime (6 Marks Depth):**

| Feature Dimension | Conventional (Physical) Crime | Computer (Cyber) Crime |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Medium of Offense** | Committed via physical weapons, bodily force, or direct physical interaction [Slide 4]. | Committed using digital hardware, software, networks, or digital devices as the primary medium [Slide 4]. |
| **Mode of Operation** | Requires direct physical presence or physical proximity at the crime scene [Slide 4]. | Operates remotely across virtual environments without physical proximity to the victim [Slide 4]. |
| **Geographical Reach & Scale** | Restricted to local physical boundaries; affects localized victims [Slide 4]. | Global reach; a single perpetrator can attack thousands of targets across international borders simultaneously [Slide 4]. |
| **Identity & Anonymity** | Physical presence leaves physical trace evidence (fingerprints, DNA, footwear marks) [Slide 4]. | Perpetrators use proxy servers, VPNs, TOR networks, and spoofed IP addresses to obscure identity [Slide 4]. |
| **Nature of Evidence** | Tangible physical items (weapons, bloodstains, documents) [Slide 5]. | Volatile digital evidence (bit-stream files, log records, registry entries, network packets) [Slide 5; Saferstein, Ch. 19]. |
| **Legal & Extradition Complexity** | Local law enforcement jurisdiction applying established criminal codes [Slide 5]. | Evolving legislation, severe jurisdictional conflicts, and complex international extradition treaties [Slide 5]. |

**1. Primary Reasons & Motivational Drivers for Cybercrime:**
* **Perceived Anonymity & Masked Identity:** The virtual nature of cyberspace allows perpetrators to hide behind pseudo-identities, encrypted VPNs, and spoofed IP addresses. This perceived anonymity lowers psychological barriers against committing illegal acts [Slide 3; Saferstein, Ch. 19].
* **Astronomical Financial Rewards:** High payout potentials from ransomware extortion, bank fraud, cryptocurrency theft, identity theft, and selling stolen data on dark web marketplaces [Slide 3; B.R. Sharma, Ch. 26].
* **Ease of Access & Automation:** Cybercriminals can launch automated exploit scripts, botnets, and phishing campaigns targeting millions of systems simultaneously with minimal physical effort or risk [Slide 3].
* **System Vulnerabilities & Organizational Opportunities:** Weak password policies, unpatched software flaws, improper network security configurations, and lack of employee cybersecurity awareness create easy attack vectors [Slide 12].
* **Disgruntled Insiders & Corporate Espionage:** Current or former employees possessing authorized credentials or internal network layout knowledge act out of revenge, sabotage, or corporate bribery [Slide 6, 12].
* **Hacktivism & Political/Ideological Motives:** Attacking government or corporate digital infrastructure to broadcast political propaganda, voice social protest, or cause economic disruption [Slide 6].

**2. Legal & Systemic Challenges Fueling Cybercrime Growth (10 Marks Depth):**
* **Jurisdictional & Cross-Border Obstacles:** Cyberattacks originate in foreign jurisdictions where extradition laws are absent or uncooperative, hampering prosecution [Slide 5, 11; B.R. Sharma, Ch. 26].
* **Low Risk of Detection & Conviction:** Digital evidence is inherently volatile and easily destroyed (wiping, encryption, bit-shifting), resulting in low conviction rates relative to physical crimes [Slide 11].

#### Question Group 1.3: Prosecutorial & Investigative Challenges in Cybercrime
* **Original PYQs:**
  1. *"Explain why prosecuting cybercrime is challenging..."* (SVKMs NMIMS MPSTME, Final Exam 2025–26, Date: 11 Dec 2025, Q7-a [part], **Marks: 6**, CO-3; BL-2) [`QP_Final-Exam_Introduction to Forensic Science(702BS0I021)_Semester V_2025-2026.pdf`, `ANS_Final-Exam_Introduction to Forensic Science(702BS0I021)_Semester V_2025-2026.pdf`, Slide 11]
  2. *"Identify the challenges faced by digital forensic."* (SVKMs NMIMS MPSTME, Sem-VI Final Exam 2023–24, Date: 02 May 2024, Q7-c, **Marks: 6**, CO-3; SO-6; BL-2) [`Introduction_to_Forensic_Science__Sem_VI__Final_Exam_2023-24_cXHk2b4kTT.pdf`, Slide 11]
* **Priority Level:** ⭐ **Important** (Core operational challenges question)

##### Model Answer (6 Marks):

**1. Six Core Challenges in Investigating and Prosecuting Cybercrimes:**
1. **Lack of Physical Evidence:** Unlike physical crime scenes containing blood, fingerprints, or weapons, digital crime scenes yield invisible binary data that can be altered or erased instantly without leaving physical traces [Slide 11].
2. **Attribution & Anonymity Barriers:** Proving *who* was sitting behind the keyboard at the exact time of the crime is difficult. IP addresses resolve to hardware locations or accounts, not human individuals [Slide 3; Nelson et al., Ch. 1].
3. **Cross-Border Jurisdictional Limitations:** Attackers execute crimes from foreign nations, requiring mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs) that are notoriously slow, politically sensitive, or non-existent [Slide 5, 11].
4. **Extreme Volatility & Anti-Forensic Techniques:** Digital data in RAM is lost upon power-off. Sophisticated criminals use anti-forensic tools, encryption, steganography, or log-wiping scripts to obscure evidence [Slide 11; Saferstein, Ch. 19].
5. **Complexity & Rapidly Evolving Technology:** The technical complexity of cloud computing, decentralized cryptocurrencies, and darknet networks often exceeds the technical understanding of traditional law enforcement officers and judicial courts [Slide 11].
6. **Judicial & Legal Admissibility Demands:** Digital evidence must strictly satisfy rigorous chain of custody protocols, hash integrity verification, and statutory certification (e.g., Section 65B Indian Evidence Act / Sec. 63 BSA 2023) [Slide 11; Nelson et al., Ch. 4].

### Topic 2: Security Breaches, Malware Typologies, & Cyber Threats

#### Question Group 2.1: Security Breaches & System Attack Lifecycle
* **Original PYQ:** *"Discuss several sorts of security breaches and how you plan to avoid such problems."* (SVKMs NMIMS MPSTME, Batch 2023–24 Re-Exam, Date: 04 Jul 2024, Q4-b, **Marks: 7**, CO-3; SO-5; BL-2) [`Introduction_to_Forensic_Science_Batch_2023-24_Re_Exam_gjfF0EzgWd.pdf`, Slide 5–6, 12]
* **Priority Level:** ⭐ **Important** (System security & lifecycle question)

##### Model Answer (7 Marks):

**1. Definition & Stages of a Digital Security Breach:**
A **security breach** is any unauthorized access to digital systems, networks, or databases to steal, alter, or destroy sensitive data [Slide 5]. Attackers execute breaches through a multi-stage lifecycle [Slide 12]:
* **Stage 1 — Reconnaissance:** Researching target vulnerabilities via port scanning, network mapping, and employee social engineering [Slide 12].
* **Stage 2 — Initial Access:** Exploiting system flaws, weak passwords, unpatched software, or deploying phishing emails to gain entry [Slide 12].
* **Stage 3 — Privilege Escalation & Execution:** Gaining administrative/root controls and executing malicious payloads [Slide 5].
* **Stage 4 — Exfiltration / Destruction:** Stealing proprietary data or deploying ransomware/wipers [Slide 5].

**2. Threat Actors & Breach Categories:**
* **Hackers:** External actors exploiting software flaws [Slide 5].
* **Malicious Insiders:** Disgruntled employees using authorized credentials to exfiltrate trade secrets [Slide 6].
* **Hacktivists:** Ideological groups launching DDoS attacks or website defacement [Slide 6].

**3. Comprehensive Prevention & Mitigation Strategy:**

| Vulnerability Vector [Slide 12] | Security Breach Problem | Defense & Prevention Plan |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Weak Passwords** | Brute-force and dictionary attacks guessing credentials. | Mandatory Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) and strong password complexity policies. |
| **Unpatched Software** | Exploitation of zero-day vulnerabilities and known software bugs. | Automated vulnerability management and prompt software patching. |
| **Lack of Employee Training** | Falling victim to phishing emails and social engineering lures. | Regular employee cybersecurity awareness training and simulated phishing drills. |
| **Insecure Configurations** | Open ports, weak firewall rules, unencrypted data transmission. | Implementing Network Firewalls, Intrusion Detection/Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS), and End-to-End Encryption. |

#### Question Group 2.2: Threat Typologies — Spamming, Phishing, Catfishing, & Cyberstalking
* **Original PYQs:**

  2. *"Explain the following terms briefly: Catfishing, spamming, Stalking, phishing."* (SVKMs NMIMS MPSTME, Sem-VI Final / Re-Exam 2024–25 / 2023–24, Date: 13 May 2025, Q4-a, **Marks: 4**, CO-2; SO-5; BL-2) [`Introduction_to_Forensic_Science__Sem_VI__Final_Exam_2024-25_and_Re_Exam_2023-24_T6b3nmwozV.pdf`, Slide 10]
* **Priority Level:** 🔥 **Very Important** (Repeated verbatim across multiple exam sessions)

##### Model Answer (1 Mark Each = 4 Marks Total):

* **Spamming:** The distribution of unsolicited bulk electronic messages (usually over email or messaging platforms) indiscriminately to large numbers of recipients for commercial advertising, scams, or spreading malware [Slide 10].
* **Phishing:** A social engineering cybercrime where attackers impersonate trustworthy entities (e.g., banks, corporate admins) via deceptive emails, spoofed websites, or messages to trick victims into revealing sensitive credentials, PINs, or financial data [Slide 10].
* **Catfishing:** The practice of creating a completely deceptive fake online persona or social media profile to trick a target individual into an artificial relationship for financial extortion, emotional manipulation, or harassment [Slide 10].
* **Cyberstalking:** The repeated use of electronic communication channels (e.g., email, social media, messaging apps, GPS tracking) to monitor, harass, threaten, or intimidate a victim, causing severe emotional distress or fear of harm [Slide 10].

#### Question Group 2.3: Legal Elements & Culpability of Stalking (*Actus Reus* & *Mens Rea*)
* **Original PYQ:** *"Based on the maxim 'Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea' explain in detail the elements of the crime with reference to stalking."* (SVKMs NMIMS MPSTME, Batch 2025–26 Re-Exam, Date: 11 Jul 2026, Q5-a, **Marks: 10**, CO-5; BL-4) [`Introduction_to_Forensic_Science_Batch_2025-26_Re_Exam_8KXL0dAZVm.pdf`, Slide 10; `Unit-2_2025 (1).pdf`]
* **Priority Level:** 🔥 **Very Important** (10-Mark legal application essay)

##### Model Answer (10 Marks):

**1. Interpretation of the Legal Maxim:**
* **Maxim:** *"Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea"* translates to *"An act does not make a person guilty unless their mind is also guilty."*
* **Core Principle:** To establish criminal liability in court, the prosecution must prove two fundamental legal elements beyond reasonable doubt:
  1. **Actus Reus:** The physical guilty act or criminal conduct.
  2. **Mens Rea:** The evil intention, guilty mind, or knowledge of wrongdoing behind the act [`Unit-2_2025 (1).pdf`].

**2. Application of Legal Elements to Cyberstalking:**

```
                            LEGAL ELEMENTS OF CYBERSTALKING
                                           │
            ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
            ▼                                                             ▼
       ACTUS REUS                                                     MENS REA
  (Physical Guilty Act)                                        (Guilty Criminal Mind)
  • Repeated electronic monitoring                             • Specific intent to intimidate
  • Persistent unwanted communications                         • Knowledge of causing fear/distress
  • Doxing / GPS location tracking                             • Reckless disregard of victim's safety
```

* **A. Actus Reus (The Physical Overt Acts of Stalking):**
  * *Repeated Unwanted Contact:* Sending persistent, continuous emails, direct messages, or comments despite clear rejection or blocking [Slide 10].
  * *Electronic Surveillance:* Tracking the victim's online activity, location check-ins, or deploying spyware/keyloggers [Slide 10].
  * *Cyber Doxing:* Publishing the victim's private contact information, home address, or private photos online to invite public harassment [Slide 8].
  * *Impersonation:* Creating fake accounts to interact with the victim's friends or family to monitor them [Slide 10].
* **B. Mens Rea (The Intentional Guilty Mind):**
  * *Wilful Intention:* The stalker acted with the conscious purpose to intimidate, cause severe emotional distress, or induce fear of bodily harm in the victim [Slide 10].
  * *Knowledge of Non-Consent:* The perpetrator knew or reasonably ought to have known that their repeated pursuit was unwanted and threatening.
  * *Recklessness:* Disregarding the obvious distress caused by persistent harassment.

**3. Statutory Framework & Penalties (Section 354-D IPC & IT Act 2000):**
* **Section 354-D of Indian Penal Code (IPC):** Explicitly defines cyberstalking as monitoring a woman's internet, email, or electronic communication activity with intent. Punishable by up to 3 years imprisonment for a first offense.
* **Section 66E / 67 of Information Technology Act 2000:** Covers violation of privacy, transmission of obscene material, and digital harassment.

* **Priority Level:** ⭐ **Important** (Broad classification essay)

##### Model Answer (7 Marks):

**1. Categorization of Computer Crimes:**
Computer crimes encompass illegal acts where computers serve as the target, tool, or venue [Slide 3]. They are categorized into four major domains [Slide 7–10; Saferstein, Ch. 19]:

1. **System Intrusion & Hacking:** Unauthorized entry into computer networks, exploiting vulnerabilities, bypassing firewalls, or installing backdoors [Slide 7].
2. **Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) Crimes:**
   * *Software Piracy:* Unauthorized copying, distribution, or commercial sale of copyrighted software and digital media [Slide 7].
   * *Cybersquatting:* Fraudulently registering internet domain names containing trademarked business names to sell them at extortionate prices [Slide 7].
   * *Trade Secret & Patent Infringement:* Theft of proprietary manufacturing formulas or industrial designs via digital exfiltration [Slide 7, 8].
3. **Computer Terrorism & Critical Infrastructure Attacks:**
   * Attacks on national power grids, water transport systems, or financial exchanges using ransomware or wipers [Slide 8].
   * *DDoS Attacks:* Flooding servers with traffic using zombie botnets to force offline outages [Slide 8].
4. **Cyber Hate Speech & Online Harassment:**
   * Cyberbullying, social media abuse, doxing, and inciting communal or ethnic violence [Slide 8, 9].
   * Fraudulent financial scams (phishing, spamming, catfishing, and cyberstalking) [Slide 10].

* **Priority Level:** 🔥 **Very Important** (Repeated across 4 separate exam papers)

##### Model Answer (6 Marks):

**1. Definition of Digital Forensics:**
Digital Forensics is the specialized branch of forensic science involving the identification, acquisition, extraction, preservation, analysis, and court presentation of digital evidence stored on electronic devices and networks in a forensically sound manner [Slide 11; Nelson et al., Ch. 1].

**2. Key Advantages & Role of Cyber Experts in Investigations:**
* **Timely Timeline Reconstruction:** Digital evidence provides precise system log timestamps (MAC times: Modified, Accessed, Created) that help reconstruct the exact sequence of events before, during, and after a crime [Slide 11].
* **Forensic Recovery of Hidden / Deleted Evidence:** Experts recover deleted files from unallocated disk space, carve fragments from slack space, and decrypt hidden partitions using specialized tools (FTK, Autopsy) [Slide 13; Saferstein, Ch. 19].
* **Perpetrator Identification & Linking:** Connecting a physical suspect to digital acts by analyzing IP address routes, MAC addresses, social media metadata, and email headers [Slide 11].
* **Preservation of Evidence Integrity:** Employing hardware write-blockers and cryptographic hashing (MD5/SHA-256) to verify that digital evidence remains unaltered from scene collection to court presentation [Slide 13; Nelson et al., Ch. 4].
* **Expert Testimony in Court:** Translating complex technical findings into understandable court reports and testifying as an expert witness under statutory evidence acts [Slide 13; B.R. Sharma, Ch. 26].

##### Model Answer (4 or 8 Marks):

**1. Tracing and Identifying the Perpetrator (Sub-question 1 - 2 Marks):**
* Experts analyze server log files, network packet headers, and social media account metadata to extract the originating **IP address** and **ISP connection logs** [Slide 11; `ANS_Final-Exam_Introduction to Forensic Science(702BS0I021)_Semester V_2025-2026.pdf`].
* By serving legal subpoenas to the ISP, investigators link the IP address and connection timestamps to the physical subscriber account, physical device MAC address, and geographical location [Saferstein, Ch. 19].

**2. Types of Extractable Social Media Evidence (Sub-question 2 - 2 Marks):**
* **Account Metadata & Activity Logs:** IP login logs, associated email addresses, registered phone numbers, and profile modification history [Slide 11].
* **Content Evidence:** Post text, attached images/videos, EXIF location metadata, private direct messages (DMs), comments, deleted post artifacts, and social graph contact connections [Slide 11].

**4. Forensic Tools to Recover Deleted Messages (Sub-question 4 - 2 Marks):**
* **FTK Imager / Autopsy:** Used for hard drive and flash storage bit-stream image acquisition, file carving, and unallocated space recovery [Slide 13].
* **Cellebrite UFED / Magnet AXIOM / Wireshark / WinHex:** Specialized mobile and network forensic suites designed to extract deleted SQLite database records, chat logs, and network artifacts [Slide 13; `ANS_Final-Exam_Introduction to Forensic Science(702BS0I021)_Semester V_2025-2026.pdf`].

### Topic 6: Cybercrimes Related to Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

#### Supplementary Practice Question 6.2 (6 Marks):
*"Explain the major forms of Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) cybercrimes, including Software Piracy, Cybersquatting, Trade Secret Theft, and Patent Infringement."*

##### Model Answer (6 Marks):

1. **Software Piracy:** The illegal copying, downloading, modification, distribution, or commercial sale of copyrighted software programs or digital media without authorization [Slide 7].
2. **Cybersquatting:** The bad-faith practice of registering, trafficking in, or using domain names identical or confusingly similar to established commercial trademarks to extort financial settlements from brand owners [Slide 7].
3. **Trade Secret Theft:** The unauthorized acquisition, copying, or digital exfiltration of confidential, commercially valuable proprietary business data (e.g., source code, chemical formulas) [Slide 8].
4. **Patent Infringement:** The unauthorized manufacture, digital distribution, or commercial use of a patented technological process or invention without a valid owner license [Slide 8].

### Topic 7: Hard Disk Forensics, Geometry, & Slack Space Analysis

#### Supplementary Practice Question 7.3 (6 Marks):
*"Explain the physical geometry of a Hard Disk Drive (HDD). Define Sector, Cluster, RAM Slack, and File Slack with a mathematical calculation example."*

##### Model Answer (6 Marks):

**1. Physical Storage Geometry of HDD:**
* **Platters, Tracks, & Sectors:** Hard disk platters are coated with magnetic material and divided into concentric rings called **tracks**. Each track is divided into arc segments called **sectors** [Slide 13; Saferstein, Ch. 19].
* **Sector:** The smallest addressable physical storage unit on a hard drive, traditionally holding **512 Bytes** of data [Saferstein, Ch. 19; Nelson et al., Ch. 5].
* **Cluster (Allocation Unit):** A group of sectors (always in multiples of 2, e.g., 4, 8, 16 sectors) treated by the Operating System as a single logical allocation unit for file storage [Saferstein, Ch. 19].

**2. Slack Space Concepts:**
* **Slack Space:** The unused physical memory space between the end of a logical file's actual data and the end of the last assigned physical cluster [Slide 13; Nelson et al., Ch. 5].
* **RAM Slack:** The unused space from the end of the logical file data to the end of that specific *sector*. The operating system pads this space with data from system RAM (potentially leaking passwords or deleted text) [Saferstein, Ch. 19].
* **File Slack (Drive Slack):** The remaining completely unused *sectors* in the cluster following the RAM slack sector [Saferstein, Ch. 19].

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────── CLUSTER (4096 Bytes = 8 Sectors) ───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
├──────────────────────────── Sector 1 ────────────────────────────┼────────── Sector 2 ──────────┼── Sectors 3 to 8 (Unused) ──┤
│ File Data (300 Bytes)           │ RAM Slack (212 Bytes)         │ File Slack (512 Bytes)      │ File Slack (3072 Bytes)     │
└─────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┘
```

## Part 3: Formula Sheet & Worked Numerical Examples

### 1. Hard Disk Storage & Slack Space Calculations

#### Mathematical Formulae:
1. **Cluster Capacity (Bytes):**
   $$	ext{Cluster Size (Bytes)} = 	ext{Sectors per Cluster} 	imes 	ext{Bytes per Sector}$$
2. **Total Assigned Physical Space (Bytes):**
   $$	ext{Allocated Space} = 	ext{Number of Assigned Clusters} 	imes 	ext{Cluster Size}$$
3. **Total Slack Space (Bytes):**
   $$	ext{Total Slack Space} = 	ext{Allocated Physical Space} - 	ext{Logical File Size}$$
4. **RAM Slack (Bytes):**
   $$	ext{RAM Slack} = 	ext{Bytes per Sector} - (	ext{Logical File Size} \pmod{	ext{Bytes per Sector}})$$
   *(Note: Applies when logical file size does not fill the last sector).*

---

#### Worked Example 3.1: Complete Slack Space & Allocation Analysis

**Problem Statement:**
A forensic analyst examines a Windows system where the hard drive file system uses **512-Byte sectors** and allocates **8 sectors per cluster**. A text file containing a suspect's crime notes has a logical file size of **1,500 Bytes**.
1. Calculate the total cluster size in Bytes.
2. Determine how many clusters are allocated to store this file.
3. Calculate the total physical space allocated on the disk.
4. Calculate the total Slack Space.
5. Determine the breakdown between RAM Slack and File Slack.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

* **Step 1: Calculate Cluster Size:**
  $$	ext{Cluster Size} = 8 	ext{ sectors/cluster} 	imes 512 	ext{ Bytes/sector} = 4,096 	ext{ Bytes}$$

* **Step 2: Determine Allocated Clusters:**
  Since file size is 1,500 Bytes and 1 cluster holds 4,096 Bytes, the file fits inside **1 Cluster**.
  $$	ext{Allocated Clusters} = 1 	ext{ Cluster}$$

* **Step 3: Calculate Total Physical Space Allocated:**
  $$	ext{Allocated Physical Space} = 1 	imes 4,096 	ext{ Bytes} = 4,096 	ext{ Bytes}$$

* **Step 4: Calculate Total Slack Space:**
  $$	ext{Total Slack Space} = 4,096 	ext{ Bytes (Allocated)} - 1,500 	ext{ Bytes (Logical Size)} = 2,596 	ext{ Bytes}$$

* **Step 5: Breakdown of RAM Slack vs. File Slack:**
  * Sector 1 holds Bytes 1 to 512 (Full).
  * Sector 2 holds Bytes 513 to 1024 (Full).
  * Sector 3 holds Bytes 1025 to 1500 (476 Bytes of data).
  * Sector 3 remaining space = $512 - 476 = 36 	ext{ Bytes}$ (**RAM Slack**).
  * Sectors 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 are completely unwritten Sectors (5 sectors total).
  * File Slack = $5 	ext{ sectors} 	imes 512 	ext{ Bytes/sector} = 2,560 	ext{ Bytes}$ (**File Slack**).
  * *Verification Check:* Total Slack = $36 	ext{ (RAM Slack)} + 2,560 	ext{ (File Slack)} = 2,596 	ext{ Bytes}$.
